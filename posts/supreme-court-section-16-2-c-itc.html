If your supplier does not pay the tax, you lose the credit

An eight-year debate on section 16(2)(c) of the CGST Act has just been closed by the Supreme Court — and not in the buyer\u2019s favour.

Bhandari Scrap Traders v. Union of India & Ors.
SLP (C) No. 23931 of 2026 and connected matters · Supreme Court of India · 24 July 2026 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 725
Sanjay Kumar and Sanjeev Sachdeva, JJ.

You hold a valid tax invoice. You received the goods. You paid your supplier the full consideration, GST included. The invoice showed up in your GSTR-2B. Your supplier then never deposited that tax with the Government.

For eight years the High Courts split both ways on what happens next. Delhi, Tripura, Karnataka and Gauhati leaned towards the buyer; Kerala, Patna, Andhra Pradesh, Bombay and Madhya Pradesh leaned towards the Revenue. That debate is now closed.

What was before the Court

Fifty-six writ petitions were heard together by the Gujarat High Court — R/SCA No. 18080 of 2023 with 55 connected matters, decided 1 May 2026 as Maruti Enterprise v. Union of India & Ors.

The petitioners were ordinary purchasing dealers: valid invoices held, goods actually received, consideration and GST paid to the supplier, credit reflected in GSTR-2A and 2B. Credit was denied for one reason alone — the supplier had not deposited the tax. There was no allegation of collusion or bogus billing.

They asked the Court to strike down section 16(2)(c) as violating Articles 14, 19(1)(g), 265 and 300A; failing that, to read it down so that it covered only fraudulent or collusive transactions.

The arguments

For the buyer

  • The law asks the impossible. The purchaser has no statutory, contractual or factual means of seeing the supplier's GSTR-3B. Lex non cogit ad impossibilia.
  • Genuineness is already tested elsewhere. Clauses (a), (aa), (b) and (ba) test the invoice, the communication and actual receipt. Clause (c) adds a variable no buyer controls.
  • Unequals are treated alike. The diligent buyer and the buyer who colluded suffer the identical consequence — hostile discrimination under Article 14.
  • The charge sits on the supplier. Section 9 places the levy on him; denying credit shifts his burden onto a faultless recipient.
  • The same supply is taxed twice, contrary to Article 265 read with the Objects clause of the CGST Bill, whose stated purpose was to remove cascading.

For the Revenue

  • Read it with section 41(2) and Rule 37A. Reverse now, re-avail the month the supplier pays. The credit is deferred, not destroyed.
  • Section 155 puts the burden on the claimant. Neither Delhi nor Tripura had examined that provision.
  • Section 53 and the IGST settlement. GST is destination-based. Allow credit on invoices alone and the originating State must transfer money it never received.
  • GST is not VAT. The DVAT Act had no counterpart to section 41(2), section 155 or Rule 37A, so the Delhi line does not transplant.
  • Credit is a concession, not a fundamental or vested right. Hardship is no ground to invalidate, and there is no equity in a taxing statute.

What the Court held

The High Court was fully justified in holding that no grounds were made out to declare Section 16(2)(c) of the CGST Act as unconstitutional or read down the provisions thereof.

The Special Leave Petitions were dismissed and the Gujarat High Court judgment affirmed. Section 16(2)(c) is neither unconstitutional nor liable to be read down. The Court noted that the Tripura High Court matters were pending, but preferred the Gujarat analysis as the more comprehensive one.

The reasoning, in six pillars

  1. The Objects clause. The Statement of Objects and Reasons speaks of credit "in respect of taxes paid". Payment is the foundation of the scheme.
  2. Read (a) to (d) together. The Revenue cannot be asked to stop at clause (b). Eligibility arises only when every condition up to clause (c) is met.
  3. Section 155. The burden of proving entitlement, right up to clause (c), is on the buyer — a section Delhi and Tripura never examined.
  4. You are not remediless. Section 41(2) with Rule 37A gives reversal followed by re-availment. Delay and hardship are not grounds to read down.
  5. Credit crosses State lines. Under VAT it never left the originating State. Under GST it does, and reading down would break the IGST settlement.
  6. A concession, strictly read. Equitable considerations are out of place in a taxing statute, and conditions attached to a concession must be strictly met.

These are paragraphs 42 to 86 of the Gujarat High Court judgment, expressly affirmed.

What still works for the buyer

The defence is narrower. It is not gone.

  1. Prove the supplier actually paid. Most disputes turn on this single fact. If the tax reached the treasury, clause (c) is satisfied and the demand fails on the facts.
  2. A mismatch is not a default. GSTR-2A is a facilitator, not a conclusive record — Bharti Airtel, Diya Agencies, Suncraft Energy. Non-reflection is not non-payment.
  3. The legacy-year circulars. Circular 183/15/2022-GST for FY 2017-18 and 2018-19, and Circular 193/05/2023-GST up to December 2021. The certificate route remains available.
  4. No enquiry against the supplier. D.Y. Beathel still gets orders set aside where the supplier was never summoned, never confronted and no recovery attempted.
  5. Penalty is severable from tax. Even where reversal is sustainable, penalty needs mens rea — absent in a bona fide purchaser. Contest it separately.

None of these decisions were overruled. Their ratio addresses procedure, natural justice and mismatch — not vires.

The part nobody is quoting

In the absence of stringent oversight, unscrupulous sellers could potentially enrich themselves at the expense of both the public exchequer and honest buyers.

Gujarat High Court, paragraph 88

The same Court that upheld the provision asked the Government to fix it: to bring legislative amendments or clarifications relieving the burden on purchasers with an honest claim; to build a real-time, technology-driven mechanism verifying supplier payments against specific invoices; and to recover promptly from erring suppliers instead of pushing buyers towards cumbersome alternate remedies.

The Court also referred approvingly to the Axel Kittel test — denial only where the buyer knew or ought to have known of the fraud — at paragraph 87, obiter.

Six things worth changing this week

  1. Reconcile GSTR-2B every month. Annual reconciliation is no longer a defensible practice.
  2. Diarise Rule 37A. Supplier's GSTR-3B not filed by 30 September of the next financial year? Reverse by 30 November, or interest runs.
  3. Put an indemnity clause in every supply agreement. Retain the tax component until the supplier files. The Court expressly endorsed this route.
  4. Rate your vendors, and act on the rating. The judgment says a bona fide buyer must exercise due diligence and stop transacting once he knows of default.
  5. Re-plead pending appeals. Any ground resting on On Quest, Arise India or Sahil Enterprises now needs rebuilding.
  6. Fight the penalty separately. Reversal of credit and imposition of penalty are not the same battle.

The takeaway

You are now expected to know your vendor's compliance as well as your own. Section 16(2)(c) survives intact; the broad bona fide purchaser defence does not. What remains is factual, procedural, and won case by case.
