FAST-DS 2026: two routes, and why the cheaper one is easy to miss

Two people, two undisclosed foreign assets. One pays ₹1,00,000. The other pays an effective 60%. Same law, same deadline — and the difference has nothing to do with how large the asset is.

Chapter IV, sections 130 to 144, Finance Act, 2026 · FAST-DS Rules, 2026 · Notification No. 114/2026, G.S.R. 732(E) dated 14 August 2026

Since 2015, the Black Money Act has treated an unreported foreign bank account exactly as it treats a deliberate offshore structure: 30% tax, penalty of up to three times the tax, and imprisonment of three to ten years on prosecution. FAST-DS is the attempt to separate the careless from the deliberate.

Who can declare

Eligibility turns on residential status, and is drawn wide enough to cover people whose status has changed over the years. A declaration may be made by a person resident in India under section 6 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 in the relevant previous year; or by a non-resident or RNOR in that year, provided he was resident when the income arose or when the asset was acquired. Someone who has since moved abroad is not shut out.

The grounds are three. The assessee never filed a return under section 139; or filed one but failed to disclose the asset or income in a return furnished before the Scheme commenced; or the asset or income escaped assessment within the meaning of section 147. Any previous year may be covered, subject to the ceilings.

One scheme, two very different prices

Which row of the section 133 Table applies has nothing to do with how large the asset is. It depends on where the money came from.

What 60% looks like

A foreign bank account of ₹60,00,000 and undisclosed foreign income of ₹20,00,000 aggregate to ₹80,00,000 — within the ceiling, so Route 1 applies. The amount payable is ₹48,00,000: ₹24 lakh of tax and ₹24 lakh as the additional amount.

What ₹1 lakh buys

A plot of land outside India acquired in 2015 out of income earned abroad while non-resident, never entered in the relevant Schedule after the assessee became resident. Value on 31 March 2026: ₹3 crore, within the ₹5 crore ceiling. Amount payable: ₹1,00,000.

Push it past the line, though, and the position changes completely. A foreign mutual fund of ₹2.5 crore plus quoted shares of ₹4 crore aggregate to ₹6.5 crore. That exceeds ₹5 crore, so the case falls outside the Table altogether and the assessee cannot declare at all.

Valuation under Rule 3

Fair market value is the higher of the cost of acquisition and the open-market price on 31 March 2026, on a report from a valuer recognised in the country where the asset is located. Where no valuation is obtained, the indexed cost of acquisition is deemed to be the fair market value.

Values are reported in rupees, converted at the RBI reference rate on the valuation date; a non-designated currency routes through the US dollar first. Under Rule 3(3), where a new asset came out of an old one, the old asset's value is reduced by the amount reinvested, so there is no double counting.

Assets transferred before 31 March 2026 take the higher of cost and sale price — or, where transferred for no or inadequate consideration, fair market value on the date of transfer.

Trap one: the bank account is measured gross

The value is the sum of every deposit since the account was opened — not the closing balance, and not the peak balance. A modest account can carry a decade of salary credits well past ₹1 crore without the holder ever having held ₹1 crore.

  • Deposits funded out of a withdrawal from the same account are excluded — but the trail has to be evidenced.
  • Where the account was already declared under Chapter VI of the Black Money Act and taxed, only deposits since that declaration count.
  • The 20% safe harbour in Rule 5(2) does not apply to bank accounts.

Trap two: it is a cliff, not a slab

At ₹1,00,00,000 the declarant pays 60% and walks away clean. At ₹1,00,00,001 he is outside the Scheme entirely — no partial relief, no scaled-down benefit, and back to 30% tax, penalty of up to 300% and prosecution risk under the Black Money Act, 2015. The same cliff sits at ₹5 crore on Route 2.

The immunity is narrower than it looks

What it covers. Further tax, penalty and prosecution under the Black Money Act, 2015 — and the declared amount is not included in total income, under section 136.

What it does not cover. FEMA, where LRS breaches and unauthorised holding survive; the PMLA; the Benami law; and prosecution under the Income-tax Act. There is no rectification, revision or set-off on completed assessments under section 137, and the amount paid is not refundable under section 138.

Where the Scheme does not apply at all

Section 140 shuts out two categories: any income or asset which directly or indirectly represents proceeds of crime, where PMLA proceedings have been initiated or are pending; and any income or asset relating to an assessment year for which Black Money Act assessment proceedings are already complete. Form 1 requires the declarant to certify that section 140 is not attracted.

Who should be looking

  • Employees holding ESOPs or RSUs in a foreign parent company, vesting quietly year after year.
  • Professionals back from overseas deputation with brokerage and retirement accounts still open.
  • NRIs who relocated to India and became resident without revisiting what Schedule FA asked of them.
  • Anyone who inherited a foreign account or holding and never reported it.
  • Students who opened accounts abroad and simply left them running.

In most of these files the tax position was clean. Only the reporting failed.

Before you file

Valuation first. Classification second. Filing is irreversible. Once Form 1 goes in, the department has a full written disclosure — and the money paid does not come back. The decision has to be taken before the form is opened.
